## 1. Bluffing and hidden-role games as research objects

### 1a. Poker and bluffing: theory and superhuman AI

- **Kuhn (1951)**, *A simplified two-person poker* [kuhn1951simplified]. Kuhn solved a three-card poker game analytically and showed that bluffing (and calling bluffs) is part of equilibrium play, so it is not irrational noise. *Relevance:* this is the classic reason to treat bluffing as a mixed strategy. Our claim/challenge subgame is a multi-player, multi-role generalisation of it.
- **Ferguson & Ferguson (1991)**, *Models for the game of Liar's Dice* [ferguson1991liars]. The paper gives game-theoretic solutions of Liar's Dice, where a claim is either challenged or overbid. *Relevance:* it is the closest classical analogue of the claim/challenge mechanic, with a binary costly-verification decision after an unverifiable claim.
- **Zinkevich et al. (2007)**, *Regret minimization in games with incomplete information*, NeurIPS [zinkevich2007cfr]. This paper introduced counterfactual regret minimisation (CFR). *Relevance:* CFR is the standard way to compute near-equilibrium bluffing and challenge frequencies. A reduced 3-player version of our game could use CFR as an equilibrium benchmark for the evolved strategies.
- **Brown & Sandholm (2018)**, Libratus, *Science* [brown2018libratus]. Libratus beat top professionals at heads-up no-limit poker using abstraction, CFR-based blueprints and subgame solving. *Relevance:* it is the reference point for imperfect-information game AI, and it shows that equilibrium bluffing can be computed in large two-player games.
- **Brown & Sandholm (2019)**, Pluribus, *Science* [brown2019pluribus]. Pluribus reached superhuman six-player poker although no Nash-equilibrium guarantee exists for more than two players. *Relevance:* our game also has 3–5 players. The multi-player case removes equilibrium guarantees, which justifies an evolutionary or empirical-game analysis instead of an equilibrium-solving one.
- **Brown et al. (2020)**, ReBeL, NeurIPS [brown2020rebel]. ReBeL combines reinforcement learning and search over public belief states, and it provably converges to equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games. *Relevance:* public-belief-state reasoning is a principled way to model how observers update on claims. It could inform a "Bayesian challenger" agent type.

### 1b. Hidden-role, social-deduction and negotiation games

- **Serrino et al. (2019)**, DeepRole, NeurIPS [serrino2019deeprole]. DeepRole combines CFR with deep value networks and outperforms humans in five-player *The Resistance: Avalon*, a game where cooperating with unknown teammates is the central challenge. *Relevance:* it is the main AI paper on hidden-role games. In our game a third-party intervener also has to infer whose side it is (implicitly) on.
- **Braverman, Etesami & Mossel (2008)**, *Mafia: a theoretical study of players and coalitions in a partial information environment*, *Ann. Appl. Probab.* [braverman2008mafia]. The paper derives analytically how the win probability of an informed minority scales with group size and with "detectives" (verification power). *Relevance:* it is a rigorous precedent for analysing win probabilities in a hidden-information group game as a function of verification capacity.
- **Bakhtin et al. (Meta FAIR) (2022)**, Cicero, *Science* [bakhtin2022cicero]. Cicero reached human-level play in seven-player Diplomacy by combining language models with strategic planning. *Relevance:* it shows that multi-player games mixing alliances and deception are frontier problems, and it motivates our focus on third parties.
- **Kramár et al. (2022)**, *Negotiation and honesty in AI methods for Diplomacy*, *Nat. Commun.* [kramar2022diplomacy]. Communities of agents are exploited by contract-breakers, and a disposition to sanction deviators restores honest cooperation. *Relevance:* it is the closest AI result to our question. Third-party sanctioning (here, challenges) can stabilise honesty, and we ask the dual question for rewarded third-party protection.
- **Peskov et al. (2020)**, *It takes two to lie*, ACL [peskov2020lie]. Using Diplomacy messages annotated by their senders, the authors show that receivers detect lies poorly. *Relevance:* it gives empirical support for modelling challenges as noisy and costly rather than reliable.
- **Sturtevant (2003)**, *A comparison of algorithms for multi-player games* [sturtevant2003multiplayer]. The paper compares max^n and paranoid search and shows that multi-player games need assumptions about how opponents treat each other. *Relevance:* it motivates making the "who does a third party side with" assumption an explicit model parameter.
- **Keane, Keyser & Kedziora (2025)**, *Strategy masking* **[preprint, arXiv:2501.05501]** [keane2025strategymasking]. The authors use **Coup** as a reinforcement-learning testbed for lying. Reward decomposition lets them suppress learned lying after training without much loss in performance. *Relevance:* it is one of very few papers that use Coup itself, and it confirms that Coup is accepted as a minimal deception environment.
- **Huynh (2026)**, *CoupVisor* **[preprint, arXiv:2608.15868]** [huynh2026coupvisor]. This is a Coup decision-support system with belief tracking over claims (role prior times cards held) and simulated comparisons of learned and heuristic players. Its headline finding is that the choice of reward drives learned behaviour. *Relevance:* it is the most recent Coup-specific work. Its claim-truthfulness estimator is a ready-made "rational challenger" heuristic, and the paper is very recent and single-authored, so it needs a caveat when cited.

> No peer-reviewed paper analyses Coup (or any Coup variant) with evolutionary game theory, and none studies a third-party block. The only Coup-specific work is preprints or course reports.

## 2. Third-party punishment, help and protection

### 2a. Third-party punishment and reputation

- **Fehr & Gächter (2002)**, *Altruistic punishment in humans*, *Nature* [fehr2002altruistic]. People pay to punish free-riders even with no future benefit, and this sustains cooperation. *Relevance:* it is the baseline for costly enforcement. Our "challenge" is a costly enforcement act whose cost is risky (losing an influence) rather than certain.
- **Fehr & Fischbacher (2004)**, *Third-party punishment and social norms*, *Evol. Hum. Behav.* [fehr2004thirdparty]. Unaffected third parties pay to punish norm violators. *Relevance:* this is the canonical "third party" paradigm. Our intervener is a third party who protects the victim instead of punishing the aggressor.
- **Jordan, Hoffman, Bloom & Rand (2016)**, *Third-party punishment as a costly signal of trustworthiness*, *Nature* [jordan2016thirdparty]. A model plus experiments show that punishers are trusted more and are more trustworthy, and that punishment as a signal is crowded out when helping is available as a more informative signal. *Relevance:* intervention in our game is costly and observable, so it can work as a signal. The coin reward is an explicit, institutional replacement for the reputational payoff in Jordan et al.
- **Raihani & Bshary (2015)**, *Third-party punishers are rewarded, but third-party helpers even more so*, *Evolution* [raihani2015helpers]. Bystanders reward third-party helpers more than third-party punishers. *Relevance:* this is the key empirical motivation for modelling rewarded third-party protection (help directed at the victim) rather than punishment.
- **Raihani, Grutter & Bshary (2010)**, *Punishers benefit from third-party punishment in fish*, *Science* [raihani2010fish]. In cleaner wrasse, males punish females who cheat clients and benefit directly from doing so. *Relevance:* it shows that third-party intervention can be self-serving. Our reward makes intervention self-serving by design, and the question is whether that is enough.
- **Batistoni, Barclay & Raihani (2022)**, *Proc. R. Soc. B* [batistoni2022thirdparty]. People invest more in third-party helping than in punishing, and more when observed. Partner-choice competition did not escalate either. *Relevance:* this is a recent Proc B paper in the journal family. It supports helping as the stronger signal and shows that observability matters, as it does in our public intervention.
- **Roos, Gelfand, Nau & Carr (2014)**, *Proc. R. Soc. B* [roos2014thirdparty]. In an agent-based model, third-party punishment evolves only with high strength of ties and low mobility, and punishment reputation alone is not enough. *Relevance:* it is the closest agent-based precedent for evolving third-party enforcement and a direct methodological template, including its negative result.
- **Chavez & Bicchieri (2013)**, *Third-party sanctioning and compensation behavior*, *J. Econ. Psychol.* [chavez2013compensation]. Third parties compensated victims of unfair offers rather than punishing proposers, and they punished mainly when compensation was not available. *Relevance:* it supports victim-directed intervention (blocking on behalf of the target) as a behaviourally natural strategy.
- **Lenfesty et al. (2024)**, *Third-party arbitration and forgiving strategies*, *Proc. R. Soc. B* [lenfesty2024arbitration]. Third-party arbitration stabilises reciprocity under perception errors, even when it is inaccurate. *Relevance:* it is recent Proc B evidence that third parties can add value as information sources under noise. In our game, failed and successful interventions also reveal information about hands.

### 2b. Bystander intervention and the volunteer's dilemma

- **Darley & Latané (1968)**, *J. Pers. Soc. Psychol.* [darley1968bystander]. Each bystander is less likely to help when more bystanders are present (diffusion of responsibility). *Relevance:* our rule allows only one intervener per action, which is a bystander problem with an explicit "first volunteer wins" structure. Intervention rates per capita may fall with table size.
- **Philpot et al. (2020)**, *Am. Psychol.* [philpot2020helped]. CCTV recordings of 219 public conflicts in Amsterdam, Cape Town and Lancaster show that at least one bystander intervenes in about 9 of 10 conflicts, and more bystanders means a higher chance that someone intervenes. *Relevance:* it is a counterweight to the classic bystander effect. Group-level intervention probability, not individual probability, is the relevant outcome, and we should report both.
- **Diekmann (1985)**, *Volunteer's dilemma*, *J. Conflict Resolut.* [diekmann1985volunteer]. In this n-player game a single costly volunteer produces a public benefit, and the equilibrium probability of volunteering falls with n. *Relevance:* intervention is formally a volunteer's dilemma with a bonus for the volunteer and a risk (being challenged) instead of a sure cost. This gives analytic predictions to test in simulation.
- **Archetti (2009)**, *Cooperation as a volunteer's dilemma*, *J. Evol. Biol.* [archetti2009volunteer]. The paper gives evolutionary (mixed-strategy) dynamics of the volunteer's dilemma and its link to public goods. *Relevance:* it provides the evolutionary-biology version of the volunteer's-dilemma analysis we would apply to the share of intervening strategies.
- **Przepiorka & Diekmann (2013)**, *Individual heterogeneity and costly punishment: a volunteer's dilemma*, *Proc. R. Soc. B* [przepiorka2013volunteer]. When punishment is a volunteer's dilemma, the actors with the lowest cost volunteer, and that solves the coordination problem. *Relevance:* in our game, the player best placed to bluff or truthfully hold a blocking role (Guardião/Embaixador) is the low-cost volunteer. This predicts who intervenes.

### 2c. Policing, coalitions and protection of the weak

- **Flack, Girvan, de Waal & Krakauer (2006)**, *Policing stabilizes construction of social niches in primates*, *Nature* [flack2006policing]. Removing a few third-party "policers" in pigtailed macaques destabilises the whole social network. *Relevance:* third-party conflict intervention is a system-level stabiliser. It motivates our population-level outcomes (survival, inequality) beyond individual payoffs.
- **Mesterton-Gibbons, Gavrilets, Gravner & Akçay (2011)**, *Models of coalition or alliance formation*, *J. Theor. Biol.* [mestertongibbons2011coalition]. This review covers game-theoretic models of when third parties join contests and on which side. *Relevance:* it frames intervention on the target's side as a transient coalition and supplies modelling conventions for side-taking.
- **Gavrilets, Duéñez-Guzmán & Vose (2008)**, *Dynamics of alliance formation and the egalitarian revolution*, *PLoS ONE* [gavrilets2008alliance]. Alliances that protect weaker individuals against dominants can produce egalitarian outcomes. *Relevance:* it directly motivates our hypothesis that rewarded intervention reduces outcome inequality (Gini) and the advantage of rich or early players.
- **DeScioli & Kurzban (2013)**, *A solution to the mysteries of morality*, *Psychol. Bull.* [descioli2013morality]. The paper proposes that moral condemnation evolved to coordinate side-taking in conflicts. *Relevance:* it is the theoretical framing for why third parties take sides with victims, against aggressors, and why the choice is strategic.
- **Song et al. (2021)**, *Third party interventions mitigate conflicts on interdependent networks*, *Appl. Math. Comput.* [song2021thirdparty]. In an evolutionary game on two coupled network layers, third parties playing a snowdrift game intervene in disputants' prisoner's dilemma and raise cooperation. *Relevance:* it is one of the few explicit evolutionary models of third-party intervention. It has no deception and no verification, which is our gap.

### 2d. Rewards versus punishment as incentives (EGT)

- **Sigmund, Hauert & Nowak (2001)**, *Reward and punishment*, *PNAS* [sigmund2001reward]. The paper analyses the evolutionary dynamics of rewarding and punishing in ultimatum-style interactions with reputation. *Relevance:* it is the canonical EGT treatment of positive versus negative incentives.
- **Chen, Sasaki, Brännström & Dieckmann (2015)**, *First carrot, then stick*, *J. R. Soc. Interface* [chen2015carrot]. Rewards work best to establish cooperation when it is rare, and punishment works best to maintain it. *Relevance:* this JRSI paper suggests the intervention reward may matter most at low intervention frequencies, which is testable as an invasion analysis.
- **García & Traulsen (2019)**, *Evolution of coordinated punishment to enforce cooperation from an unbiased strategy space*, *J. R. Soc. Interface* [garcia2019coordinated]. Including all strategies, including antisocial and opportunistic ones, shows that punishment institutions emerge but are limited by opportunists. *Relevance:* it is a JRSI precedent for using an unrestricted strategy space. We should include "opportunistic interveners" who intervene only when bluffing is cheap.
- **Song, Shen, Capraro & Han (2026)**, *Non-participant externalities reshape the evolution of altruistic punishment*, *J. R. Soc. Interface* [song2026nonparticipant]. When non-participants can affect the public good positively or negatively, the conditions for altruistic punishment shift. *Relevance:* this very recent JRSI paper parallels our Observers (eliminated players who stay at the table) and is useful for positioning and journal style.

## 3. Evolution of deception and honesty; costly verification

### 3a. Honest signalling theory

- **Zahavi (1975)** [zahavi1975handicap] and **Grafen (1990)** [grafen1990handicap]. These are the handicap principle and its formalisation: honest signals are maintained by differential costs. *Relevance:* the classical baseline. In our game a claim costs nothing to make, and honesty depends on the cost of being *caught*, which is the post-Zahavi view.
- **Penn & Számadó (2020)**, *Biol. Rev.* [penn2020handicap]. The paper critiques the handicap principle and argues that honesty does not require costly signals at equilibrium. *Relevance:* it supports framing honesty in our game through verification and punishment costs, not signal costs.
- **Lachmann, Számadó & Bergstrom (2001)**, *Cost and conflict in animal signals and human language*, *PNAS* [lachmann2001cost]. Honest communication can be stabilised by social punishment of liars, so costs are paid by dishonest signallers off equilibrium. *Relevance:* this is the theoretical core of our deception side. A challenge is exactly an off-equilibrium punishment of false claims, and our claims are cheap talk backed by challengeable verification.
- **Számadó (2000)**, *Cheating as a mixed strategy in a simple model of aggressive communication*, *Anim. Behav.* [szamado2000cheating]. Bluffing in threat displays can persist as a stable mixed strategy alongside honest signalling. *Relevance:* it predicts a stable interior bluffing frequency, the evolutionary counterpart of Kuhn's poker result.
- **Crawford & Sobel (1982)**, *Strategic information transmission*, *Econometrica* [crawford1982cheaptalk]. This is cheap talk: with conflicting interests, costless messages convey only coarse information. *Relevance:* role claims in Coup-like games are cheap talk, and challenges add verification on top.

### 3b. Lie detection and the economics of verification

- **Bond & DePaulo (2006)**, *Accuracy of deception judgments*, *Pers. Soc. Psychol. Rev.* [bond2006deception]. In a meta-analysis, people detect lies at about 54%, barely above chance. *Relevance:* it justifies modelling challengers without privileged cues. Challenge decisions must rest on public information (card counting, base rates, coins), not lie detection.
- **Townsend (1979)**, *Costly state verification*, *J. Econ. Theory* [townsend1979verification]. Optimal contracts verify reports only when verification pays. *Relevance:* it is the economic framing of the challenge, where a verifier pays a cost to audit a claim. Our challenge cost is stochastic and asymmetric (the challenger loses an influence if the claim is true).
- **Avenhaus, von Stengel & Zamir (2002)**, *Inspection games*, *Handbook of Game Theory* [avenhaus2002inspection]. This review covers inspector–inspectee games with mixed equilibria in violation and inspection rates. *Relevance:* bluff-versus-challenge is an inspection game. The standard result that inspection rates, not violation rates, respond to the violator's penalty is directly testable in our model.
- **Andreozzi (2004)**, *Rewarding policemen increases crime*, *Public Choice* [andreozzi2004policemen]. In a variant of the inspection game where the inspector moves first (as a Stackelberg leader), raising the inspector's incentive to enforce *increases* the frequency of infractions. This adds to the already counterintuitive comparative statics of the standard mixed equilibrium. *Relevance:* it is a perverse-incentive prediction we should test for the intervention bonus. A larger reward for interveners might *increase* bluffed interventions and so change challenge rates in the opposite direction from naive expectations.

### 3c. Evolutionary models of lying and deception (incl. recent J R Soc Interface / Proc B)

- **McNally & Jackson (2013)**, *Cooperation creates selection for tactical deception*, *Proc. R. Soc. B* [mcnally2013deception]. Conditional cooperation selects for deceivers who weaken cheater detection. *Relevance:* rewarding protectors (a conditional cooperative act) should likewise select for bluffed protection. That is our core tension.
- **Capraro, Perc & Vilone (2019)**, *The evolution of lying in well-mixed populations*, *J. R. Soc. Interface* [capraro2019lying]. In a Monte Carlo study of sender–receiver games, the stationary frequencies of liars and believers depend on lie type (black, white, spiteful). *Relevance:* it is a direct JRSI precedent for evolutionary lying models and a style template. Bluffed interventions are "Pareto white lies": they benefit the intervener (reward) and the target (protected).
- **Sarkadi et al. (2021)**, *The evolution of deception*, *R. Soc. Open Sci.* [sarkadi2021deception]. Simulations of knowledge-sharing public-goods games with deceptive agents (built on interpersonal deception and truth-default theory) show that deception erodes cooperation. Cooperation is restored by institutions that investigate and regulate both defection and deception case by case, in a decentralised way. *Relevance:* it is the closest Royal Society model coupling deception with decentralised verification (policing) in a multi-player setting.
- **Liu, Shao, Wang & Wu (2026)**, *Evolution of cooperation in psychological games with costly information*, *J. R. Soc. Interface* [liu2026costlyinfo]. Paying to learn others' true values interacts with cooperation in donation games. *Relevance:* this is very recent JRSI work making information acquisition costly. Our challenge is costly information acquisition with a stochastic penalty.

## 4. Evolutionary-game-theory methods for multi-player games

- **Taylor & Jonker (1978)**, *Math. Biosci.* [taylor1978replicator]. This introduced replicator dynamics. *Relevance:* it is used for infinite-population dynamics over a heuristic payoff table of meta-strategies.
- **Nowak, Sasaki, Taylor & Fudenberg (2004)**, *Nature* [nowak2004finite]. The paper covers fixation probabilities in the finite-population Moran process (the "one-third law"). *Relevance:* it is the stochastic finite-population counterpart, natural for small strategy pools.
- **Traulsen, Pacheco & Nowak (2007)**, *J. Theor. Biol.* [traulsen2007pairwise]. This is the pairwise-comparison (Fermi) update with a selection-intensity parameter. *Relevance:* it is the standard imitation dynamic for agent-based social-learning versions, and selection intensity should be swept.
- **Fudenberg & Imhof (2006)**, *J. Econ. Theory* [fudenberg2006imitation]. In the small-mutation limit, the dynamics reduce to a Markov chain over monomorphic states. *Relevance:* this makes a full analysis of many meta-strategies tractable (it is the basis of α-Rank-style stationary distributions).
- **Gokhale & Traulsen (2014)**, *Evolutionary multiplayer games*, *Dyn. Games Appl.* [gokhale2014multiplayer]. This review covers replicator and finite-population dynamics when payoffs come from n-player interactions. *Relevance:* our games have 3–5 seats, so payoffs must be computed from multinomial group compositions, not pairwise matches.
- **Hilbe, Wu, Traulsen & Nowak (2014)**, *Cooperation and control in multiplayer social dilemmas*, *PNAS* [hilbe2014multiplayer]. The paper characterises strategies and group-size effects in repeated n-player dilemmas. *Relevance:* it gives a group-size scaling template (we vary n = 3, 4, 5).
- **Axelrod & Hamilton (1981)**, *Science* [axelrod1981cooperation]. This is the computer tournament plus ecological (replicator) analysis of strategies. *Relevance:* it is the original tournament-then-evolve methodology we would reuse with hand-designed bluffing and intervention archetypes.
- **Wellman, Tuyls & Greenwald (2025)**, *Empirical game theoretic analysis: a survey*, *JAIR* [wellman2025egta]; **Tuyls et al. (2020)**, *Bounds and dynamics for EGTA*, *Auton. Agents Multi-Agent Syst.* [tuyls2020egta]. EGTA estimates a meta-game payoff table by simulating strategy profiles, then analyses equilibria and dynamics. Tuyls et al. give sample-complexity bounds for how many simulations are needed. *Relevance:* this is the core pipeline for us, and the bounds tell us how many games per profile to simulate.
- **Ponsen, Tuyls, Kaisers & Ramon (2009)**, *An evolutionary game-theoretic analysis of poker strategies*, *Entertain. Comput.* [ponsen2009poker]. It applies heuristic payoff tables and replicator dynamics to simulated poker strategies. *Relevance:* it is the direct precedent for EGTA of a bluffing card game.
- **Omidshafiei et al. (2019)**, *α-Rank: multi-agent evaluation by evolution*, *Sci. Rep.* [omidshafiei2019alpharank]. α-Rank ranks strategies by their mass in the stationary distribution of a Markov–Conley-chain evolutionary process, and it scales to many-player, general-sum games. *Relevance:* our games are many-player and general-sum, where Nash analysis is weak, so α-Rank is a good fit for ranking intervention and bluffing strategies.
- **Lanctot et al. (2019)**, *OpenSpiel* **[preprint, arXiv:1908.09453]** [lanctot2019openspiel]. This open-source framework covers imperfect-information games, CFR, EGT and α-Rank. *Relevance:* it is a practical implementation substrate, either for implementing the game or for checking our own CFR and α-Rank code.
- **Grimm et al. (2020)**, *ODD protocol, second update*, *JASSS* [grimm2020odd]. This is the standard for describing agent-based models. *Relevance:* JRSI reviewers expect ODD-style model descriptions for agent-based models, so we should put an ODD in the supplementary material.
- **ten Broeke, van Voorn & Ligtenberg (2016)**, *Which sensitivity analysis method should I use for my agent-based model?*, *JASSS* [tenbroeke2016sensitivity]. This guide compares OFAT, regression and variance-based (Sobol) sensitivity analysis for ABMs. *Relevance:* it is the template for the parameter sweep over reward, challenge penalty, deck composition and player count.

## 5. Games as models, game balance and outcome inequality

- **Allen et al. (2024)**, *Using games to understand the mind*, *Nat. Hum. Behav.* [allen2024games]. The authors argue that games are tractable, rich environments for behavioural and cognitive science. *Relevance:* this justifies a recreational game as a "minimal model" in a science journal.
- **Browne & Maire (2010)**, *Evolutionary game design*, *IEEE TCIAIG* [browne2010evolutionary]. Their system evolved board-game rule sets, scored by automated self-play quality criteria such as balance. *Relevance:* it is the precedent for quantifying a rule's effect (here, Intervenção) through self-play statistics.
- **Isaksen, Gopstein, Togelius & Nealen (2018)**, *Exploring game space via parameter tuning and survival analysis*, *IEEE Trans. Games* [isaksen2018gamespace]. Simulated agents and survival analysis map how rule parameters shape difficulty and length. *Relevance:* it is a methods template for survival curves (time to elimination) across rule variants.
- **Jaffe et al. (2012)**, *Evaluating competitive game balance with restricted play*, AIIDE [jaffe2012balance]. Balance is measured by restricting agents' strategies and comparing win rates. *Relevance:* we can compute "intervention-restricted" versus unrestricted play to measure what the rule contributes.
- **Hauser, Hilbe, Chatterjee & Nowak (2019)**, *Social dilemmas among unequals*, *Nature* [hauser2019unequals]. This is direct reciprocity among players unequal in endowment and productivity. Extreme inequality prevents cooperation, but some endowment inequality aligned with productivity can be necessary for it, while misalignment makes cooperation collapse. *Relevance:* it frames the inequality outcome. Coins are unequal endowments, and intervention transfers protection toward targets.
- **Drăgulescu & Yakovenko (2000)**, *Statistical mechanics of money*, *Eur. Phys. J. B* [dragulescu2000money]. Random pairwise transfers produce Boltzmann–Gibbs wealth distributions. *Relevance:* it is a null model for the coin distribution under random play. Gini coefficients should be compared against this baseline.
- **Utt & Fort (2002)**, *Pitfalls to measuring competitive balance with Gini coefficients*, *J. Sports Econ.* [utt2002gini]. Because league play is zero-sum, naive win-percentage Gini coefficients are misleading. The authors recommend comparing dispersion against its idealised (perfectly balanced) value. *Relevance:* it is a methodological caution. Gini over win counts in a zero-sum multi-player game must be benchmarked against the value expected under perfectly balanced play for the same n and number of games.

## Gap and positioning

1. **Third-party *protection* is under-modelled compared with third-party *punishment*.** The evolutionary literature on third parties is dominated by punishment (Fehr & Fischbacher 2004; Jordan et al. 2016; Roos et al. 2014; García & Traulsen 2019). Experiments consistently show that people prefer helping or compensating the victim (Chavez & Bicchieri 2013; Raihani & Bshary 2015; Batistoni et al. 2022), but formal models of victim-directed third-party defense are scarce (Song et al. 2021 is an exception, without deception).
2. **Existing third-party models assume that interveners' competence and type are observable.** In our game the intervener's *ability* to protect is itself a claim that may be a bluff and can be verified at a cost. Deception is therefore built into the protective act. We are not aware of an evolutionary model in which third-party help is (a) rewarded, (b) potentially fraudulent and (c) subject to costly, risky verification by any bystander. McNally & Jackson (2013) predict that rewarding a conditional cooperative act selects for deceptive mimics, and our model is a concrete test of that prediction for protection.
3. **Verification is modelled as an inspection game with a participation constraint.** Classic inspection-game results (Avenhaus et al. 2002; Andreozzi 2004) predict counterintuitive responses of violation and inspection rates to rewards. These results have not been examined in a setting where (i) many potential inspectors face a volunteer's dilemma (Diekmann 1985; Przepiorka & Diekmann 2013) and (ii) the inspected act is prosocial (protection), not antisocial.
4. **Hidden-role games are studied as AI benchmarks, not as models of social evolution.** Poker and Avalon/Diplomacy research (Brown & Sandholm 2019; Serrino et al. 2019; Bakhtin et al. 2022) aims at strong play. EGT analyses of games exist for poker (Ponsen et al. 2009) but not for Coup-like hidden-role games, and none for a rule that creates a third-party role. Coup appears only in preprints (Keane et al. 2025; Huynh 2026).
5. **Distributional outcomes.** Alliance models predict that protecting the weak flattens hierarchies (Gavrilets et al. 2008; Flack et al. 2006). No study has measured how a protection rule changes elimination order, outcome Gini and first-player advantage in a multi-player game of deception. Note that in the rulebook the previous winner starts the next game, so first-player advantage compounds across games.

**One-sentence positioning:** *We use a minimal hidden-role bluffing game to ask whether rewarding third parties for defending victims evolves and stabilises when the defenders' ability to protect can be faked and checking it is costly and risky. We characterise the evolutionary regimes (honest protection, fraudulent protection, no protection) and their consequences for survival, inequality and positional advantage.*

---

## Candidate research questions / hypotheses (testable via simulation)

**H1 (reward threshold for intervention).** The share of intervening strategies at evolutionary equilibrium rises non-linearly with the intervention reward *r*. There is a threshold *r\** below which intervention is driven out (volunteer's-dilemma logic: cost of exposure × probability of being challenged > expected benefit). Above *r\**, intervention persists at an interior frequency.
*Test:* EGTA meta-game over archetypes {never intervene, honest-only interveners, always intervene (bluff allowed)} × {challenge rarely, challenge by belief, challenge often}. Analyse it with replicator dynamics and α-Rank across *r* ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}, holding challenge penalties fixed.

**H2 (rewarding protectors breeds fraudulent protection, an inspection-game reversal).** As *r* rises, the bluffed share of interventions increases (McNally & Jackson 2013). In a mixed equilibrium the challenge rate against interventions, not the bluff rate, adjusts to the penalty, and the probability that a protection is genuine may *fall* with *r* (an Andreozzi-type perverse-incentive effect).
*Test:* measure P(intervention is truthful), challenge rate on interventions, and exposure rate as functions of *r* and of the challenge-loss penalty.

**H3 (verification cost shapes the honesty regime).** Making challenges costlier (for example, a coin fee in addition to the influence risk) shifts the population from an "honest-protection" regime to a "fraudulent-protection" regime. There is a region of bistability where outcomes depend on initial conditions.
*Test:* a 2-D phase diagram over (*r*, challenge cost) with the stationary distribution from small-mutation Moran dynamics, plus checks of basins of attraction under replicator dynamics.

**H4 (protection reduces inequality and positional advantage, with diminishing returns in group size).** Allowing Intervenção (compared with the baseline Coup-like rules) lowers the Gini coefficient of final coins and survival time, lowers first-seat win-rate excess, and delays first elimination. Per-capita intervention falls with n = 3→5 (bystander and volunteer's-dilemma effect), while the probability that someone intervenes stays flat or rises (Philpot et al. 2020).
*Test:* paired simulations with the same seeds, rule on versus off. Report Gini against the balanced-play benchmark (Utt & Fort 2002), seat-wise win rates with confidence intervals, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves.

**H5 (who intervenes: role-holding as the low-cost volunteer).** Interventions are carried out disproportionately by players who truthfully hold a blocking role (Guardião/Embaixador), as the low-cost-volunteer result predicts (Przepiorka & Diekmann 2013). That honest-holder share falls as *r* rises.
*Test:* log the role holdings of interveners and condition on the public card count.

## Methods we should borrow (concrete)

1. **Empirical game-theoretic analysis (EGTA) pipeline** [wellman2025egta; tuyls2020egta; ponsen2009poker]:
   (a) define 6–10 interpretable meta-strategies (parameterised by bluff propensity, challenge threshold on the posterior probability that a claim is false, intervention propensity, and target-selection rule);
   (b) build a **heuristic payoff table** for every multiset of strategies at n = 3, 4, 5 seats, with seat order randomised and recorded;
   (c) use the Tuyls et al. (2020) bounds to choose the number of games per profile (aim for a payoff standard error below about 0.01 of the win probability);
   (d) analyse with replicator dynamics [taylor1978replicator; gokhale2014multiplayer] and with **α-Rank** [omidshafiei2019alpharank], then report both.
2. **Finite-population stochastic dynamics.** Use Moran or pairwise-comparison (Fermi) updating [nowak2004finite; traulsen2007pairwise] with a sweep over selection intensity β, plus the small-mutation embedded Markov chain [fudenberg2006imitation] to get stationary distributions and transition graphs between monomorphic states. This is a JRSI-standard figure.
3. **Unbiased strategy space** [garcia2019coordinated]. Include opportunistic and antisocial variants (for example, "intervene only when bluffing", "challenge only interveners") so the result does not depend on a curated strategy set.
4. **Equilibrium benchmark on a reduced game.** Solve a 3-player, reduced-deck, single-steal-round version with CFR [zinkevich2007cfr] (for example through OpenSpiel [lanctot2019openspiel]), and compare the evolved frequencies of bluffing, challenging and intervening with the CFR strategy. For 3 players CFR has no Nash guarantee [brown2019pluribus], so present the comparison as approximate.
5. **Belief-based challenger.** Use a Bayesian posterior over each claimant's hand from public information (deck composition, revealed cards, cards held), in the spirit of public-belief-state reasoning [brown2020rebel] and of the CoupVisor estimator [huynh2026coupvisor]. Challengers act when the posterior of a lie exceeds a threshold θ, and θ is an evolvable trait.
6. **Analytic sub-models.** (i) An n-player volunteer's dilemma for intervention with reward *r*, exposure cost and challenge probability, giving closed-form equilibrium intervention probability as a function of n [diekmann1985volunteer; archetti2009volunteer]. (ii) A 2×2 inspection game, intervener (truthful or bluff) versus challenger (challenge or not), giving the Andreozzi comparative statics [avenhaus2002inspection; andreozzi2004policemen]. Validate the ABM against both.
7. **Outcome metrics and balance diagnostics.** Use the Gini of final coins and of survival time against the balanced-play benchmark [utt2002gini]; a random-transfer null model [dragulescu2000money]; seat-wise win rates and first-player excess with bootstrap confidence intervals; Kaplan–Meier survival curves to first and second influence loss [isaksen2018gamespace]; and restricted-play comparisons with the rule on versus off [jaffe2012balance].
8. **Reporting and robustness.** Write an ODD description in the ESM [grimm2020odd]; run global sensitivity analysis with Sobol indices over (*r*, challenge penalty, deck composition, n, β, mutation rate) [tenbroeke2016sensitivity]; use paired seeds for rule-on versus rule-off contrasts; and archive the code and data with a DOI.
9. **Tournament framing for communication.** Present an Axelrod-style round-robin tournament of archetypes first, then the ecological and evolutionary dynamics [axelrod1981cooperation], so the result is intuitive before the formal analysis.
